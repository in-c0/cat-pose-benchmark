# A1 preliminary result — CatMeows context prediction under held-out cats

**Date:** 2026-08-21  
**Status:** preliminary public research result; not peer-reviewed  
**Thread:** A1 — vocalisation acoustics  
**Dataset:** CatMeows v1.0.2, DOI `10.5281/zenodo.4008297`

## Summary

There is measurable acoustic signal related to the three CatMeows elicitation contexts, but this first group-aware experiment does **not** support a strong claim that context can be robustly inferred from vocal acoustics alone for unseen cats or households.

Under leave-one-cat-out evaluation, simple acoustic classifiers improve balanced discrimination over prevalence/majority prediction. The strongest simple discriminative scores are about 0.51 balanced accuracy and 0.51 macro F1 versus 0.33 and 0.22 for the prevalence baseline.

However, probability quality is much less convincing. The interpretable acoustic model obtains log loss `1.070`, essentially no improvement over the training-prevalence prior at `1.066`, although its multiclass Brier score improves from `0.644` to `0.611`. MFCC and combined-feature models are substantially more overconfident and have worse log loss.

The unsupervised result is more striking: stable acoustic clusters are associated much more strongly with **cat, owner and breed** than with elicitation context. In this dataset, acoustic structure appears to carry strong individual/household information. A cluster should therefore not be renamed as an `intent` or context state without substantially stronger controls.

This is a mixed/negative result by design. It narrows what A1 should claim and what the next experiment must control.

## A1.0 — supervised context baselines

### Primary: leave one cat out

Each cat is held out in turn. The held-out cat contributes no clips to preprocessing or training for that fold.

| Model | Balanced accuracy | Macro F1 | Log loss ↓ | Multiclass Brier ↓ | Top-label ECE ↓ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Majority | 0.3333 | 0.2229 | 17.9399 | 0.9955 | 0.4977 |
| Training prevalence prior | 0.3333 | 0.2229 | **1.0655** | 0.6436 | 0.2437 |
| Duration + F0 | 0.4436 | 0.4272 | 1.0632 | 0.6466 | **0.0602** |
| Interpretable acoustics | 0.5111 | 0.5036 | 1.0700 | **0.6109** | 0.1772 |
| MFCC | **0.5148** | **0.5105** | 1.5274 | 0.7137 | 0.2907 |
| All acoustic features | 0.4993 | 0.4955 | 1.7976 | 0.7578 | 0.3275 |

### Interpretation

There are two different stories depending on which metric is examined.

**Discrimination:** acoustic features beat the simple prevalence/majority baseline on balanced accuracy and macro F1. The best simple model reaches about 0.515 balanced accuracy, well above 0.333 chance-balanced performance.

**Probability quality:** richer models do not produce reliable probabilities under held-out cats. In particular, MFCC and all-acoustic models gain discrimination while sharply worsening log loss, Brier score and ECE. This is a classic warning against presenting only accuracy.

The interpretable acoustic model is the most defensible compromise in this first run. Compared with the prevalence prior, it improves multiclass Brier score (`0.611` vs `0.644`) and discrimination, but its log loss is effectively unchanged/slightly worse (`1.070` vs `1.066`). Across the 21 held-out cats it improves balanced accuracy on 13 cats, Brier score on 14, and log loss on 12; performance is highly heterogeneous.

For example, the all-acoustic model's held-out-cat balanced accuracy ranges from approximately `0.23` to `0.95`. That variation is too large to describe the current model as a reliable cross-cat context decoder.

### Secondary sensitivity: leave one owner out

This analysis is stricter with respect to household/owner leakage but is statistically fragile because owner groups are uneven and at least one owner group contains only one clip. It is therefore a sensitivity diagnostic, not a headline generalisation estimate.

| Model | Balanced accuracy | Macro F1 | Log loss ↓ | Multiclass Brier ↓ | Top-label ECE ↓ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Majority | 0.3333 | 0.2229 | 17.9399 | 0.9955 | 0.4977 |
| Training prevalence prior | 0.3333 | 0.2229 | **1.0579** | **0.6385** | 0.1630 |
| Duration + F0 | 0.4477 | 0.4249 | 1.0787 | 0.6594 | **0.0730** |
| Interpretable acoustics | 0.4778 | 0.4725 | 1.1808 | 0.6429 | 0.2143 |
| MFCC | 0.4417 | 0.4413 | 2.0313 | 0.8384 | 0.3719 |
| All acoustic features | **0.4897** | **0.4844** | 2.0627 | 0.7940 | 0.3423 |

The cross-owner result reinforces the calibration warning. Rich feature sets can still improve class discrimination while producing substantially worse probability scores.

## A1.1 — unsupervised acoustic structure

For each feature family, K-means was run for every `k=2..8`. We report silhouette, repeated-80%-subsample stability using mean pairwise adjusted Rand index (ARI), and adjusted mutual information (AMI) between clusters and metadata.

No automatic `best_k` or semantic label is assigned.

### Interpretable acoustics

The most stable solution is `k=3`:

- stability ARI: **0.8203**;
- silhouette: **0.1501**;
- AMI with context: **0.0105**;
- AMI with cat ID: **0.2585**;
- AMI with owner ID: **0.3094**;
- AMI with breed: **0.4453**.

The largest context association over `k=2..8` is still only about **0.0733**.

### MFCC

The most stable solution is `k=2`:

- stability ARI: **0.9509**;
- silhouette: **0.1117**;
- AMI with context: **0.0060**;
- AMI with cat ID: **0.2501**;
- AMI with owner ID: **0.3170**;
- AMI with breed: **0.5698**.

Across `k=2..8`, the largest context AMI is about **0.0870**, while cat/owner associations become much larger (up to roughly **0.47/0.49**).

### All acoustic features

The most stable solution is `k=2`:

- stability ARI: **0.9462**;
- silhouette: **0.1109**;
- AMI with context: **0.0049**;
- AMI with cat ID: **0.2719**;
- AMI with owner ID: **0.3548**;
- AMI with breed: **0.6442**.

The largest context AMI observed is about **0.0913** at `k=6`, where cat and owner AMI are approximately **0.4254** and **0.4673** respectively.

### A1.1 conclusion

The clusters are reproducible enough to be real acoustic structure, but the metadata association says that structure is mostly **not the three experimental contexts**. Breed, owner and individual identity are much stronger correlates.

This supports a key programme decision: discover latent acoustic states first, but do not automatically interpret a stable cluster as a communicative meaning. It may instead represent anatomy, individual vocal signature, recording/household conditions, or correlated factors.

## Relation to the 2019 result

Ntalampiras et al. (2019), *Automatic Classification of Cat Vocalizations Emitted in Different Contexts*, reported a best recognition rate of **95.94%** for a DAG-HMM under a ten-fold cross-validation protocol on an earlier 448-vocalisation dataset.

Our result is **not a failed numerical replication of 95.94%** because several important things differ:

- their classifier and features differ from this simple baseline ladder;
- their paper reports 448 vocalisations, whereas public CatMeows v1.0.2 contains 440;
- their experiment used ten-fold cross-validation; this A1 result deliberately uses subject-grouped leave-one-cat-out as the primary split;
- the paper does not state in the reported ten-fold description that cats were kept disjoint between training and testing.

Therefore the performance gap cannot be attributed to one cause. It does, however, make the generalisation question impossible to ignore. With strong cat/owner/breed structure visible in A1.1, future context-classification claims should report subject- and household-grouped evaluation in addition to ordinary random/fold evaluation.

## What this does and does not show

### Supported by this run

- Cat vocalisations contain acoustic information correlated with the supplied elicitation context beyond a majority/prevalence decision rule under held-out cats.
- Simple discriminative accuracy can overstate usefulness when probability calibration/generalisation is poor.
- Acoustic structure in CatMeows strongly reflects individual/owner/breed factors.
- A group-aware evaluation changes the question from “can clips from these contexts be separated?” to “does the signal transfer to a cat/household not used for training?”

### Not supported by this run

- literal translation of meows;
- a universal mapping from acoustic cluster to intent;
- robust context decoding for arbitrary unseen cats;
- causal evidence that the context caused the observed acoustic differences;
- a health, pain, welfare or emotion classifier;
- a claim that audio alone is sufficient for feline intent inference.

## Decision

**A1 stays active, but the next step is not to scale the same context classifier.**

The more informative directions are:

1. **personalisation:** quantify how little within-cat adaptation data is required to outperform the cross-cat population model;
2. **context + audio ablation:** test whether audio adds information beyond routine/location/object priors on naturalistic episodes;
3. **prospective outcomes:** predict what the cat does next rather than using elicitation context as a proxy for intent;
4. **multimodal pairing:** combine audio with V1 visual evidence only on episodes with synchronized provenance;
5. **latent-state validation:** retain acoustic clusters as unlabeled states until they predict repeatable behaviour or intervention outcomes.

The current evidence argues for a personalised multimodal temporal model more strongly than it argues for a standalone universal meow classifier.
